Who builds AI models?

In about ten years, the AI models that matter went from being built mostly by universities to being built by a handful of US and Chinese companies. So where AI goes next depends on a few firms, and on chip policy between two governments.

By Praneel Khiantani, Amy Munoz and Ashley Zheng

01

Hook

Universities used to build the AI everyone talked about.

Frame 1

In 2014, universities built most of the notable AI models. In 2026, they built none.

In 2014, universities working on their own built 21 of the 33 most notable AI models. So far in 2026 they have built 0 of 84.

02

Rising insights

Four shifts, one after another.

Frame 2 · Shift one

Industry took over

Models built only by universities fell from 46% of notable models in 2012 to 2016 (66 of 143) to 2% in 2024 to 2026 (5 of 289). Models built only by companies went from 34% (49 of 143) to 90% (261 of 289).

46% → 2%share built by universities alone, 2012–16 vs 2024–26

Frame 3 · Shift two

One reason is cost

The most expensive training run in the data went from about $2 million (GPT-3, 2020) to about $388 million (Grok 4, 2025), in 2023 dollars. Only about 1 in 5 models has a recorded cost, so the real top is probably even higher.

$2M → $388Mmost expensive training run, 2020 vs 2025

Frame 4 · Shift three

The field is also narrowing

119 labs released at least one notable model in 2023. Only 43 did in 2025, and just five of them (OpenAI, Google, Alibaba, Anthropic and xAI) worked on 59 of that year's 107 models.

119 → 43labs releasing a notable model, 2023 vs 2025

5 labs worked on 59 of 2025's 107 notable models.

Frame 5 · Shift four

It is now a two-country race, and China's share kept growing after the US cut off its best chips

Each line is one group's share of the notable AI models published that year. In 2012 to 2016, 59% came from teams with a US developer and no Chinese one, and 8% from Chinese teams (85 and 11 of 143). In 2024 to 2026 it was 57% and 32% (166 and 92 of 289).

Counting joint US + China models too, the two countries went from 71% to 91% of all notable models.

China's line is the one that moved. Its share stayed under 15% in every year before 2021. So far in 2026 it is 35% (29 of 84).

It kept rising after October 7, 2022, when the US limited exports of advanced AI chips to China. China built 13% of notable models in the two years before (21 of 159), 22% in the two years after (47 of 211) and 32% in the two years after that (71 of 222).

This counts models, not how good they are or how much computing went into them. It doesn't prove the controls failed, since a lot else changed at the same time.

Frame 6 · The policy lever

Export controls move markets, but China's model output kept climbing

Across the 22 US export-control events in our list, Nvidia's stock moved anywhere from −11% to +10% in the 5 trading days after. The median move was about −2%.

Main message

04

What can change

The trend isn't fixed.

Frame 8

What can change this

  1. Give universities shared public computing power. The NSF's National AI Research Resource pilot, started in January 2024, is one example. It lets researchers use computers they couldn't afford alone, so open research can still build frontier models.
  2. Judge chip policy by what it changes in who builds AI, not only by how chip stocks react.

How we counted

  • Models. Epoch's notable list: a model beat the previous best result on a task, was highly cited, was widely used, cost a lot to train, or mattered historically. 931 models, 2012 to Sep 30, 2026.
  • Sectors come from Epoch's organization type. A university and a company together is its own sector.
  • Labs. A model built by several labs counts once for each. One company's units count as one lab (Google Brain and DeepMind are Google, Facebook AI Research is Meta, Microsoft Research is Microsoft). A lab's country is the one it is listed under on most of its models.
  • Countries. Each model counts once: US, China, US + China together, or rest of world. Hong Kong counts as China.
